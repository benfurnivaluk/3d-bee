7. Inspections: an end-to-end method
A routine inspection is a brief, purposeful examination to establish whether the colony is healthy, queen-
right, fed, adequately housed and approaching a condition that requires intervention. Most inspections do
not require finding the queen. Fresh eggs are good evidence that she was laying recently. Opening
repeatedly during cold, windy or wet weather can chill brood and disrupt the cluster, so adapt frequency
to season and conditions.

Step-by-step opening and closing
  1.    Plan first. Check the weather, read the prior inspection note and decide the question you must
        answer. Prepare spare supers, nuc boxes or frames if the previous note suggests they may be
        needed.
  2.    Observe without opening. Note entrance traffic, pollen loads, fighting, wasps, dead bees,
        disoriented or crawling workers and whether other hives behave differently.
  3.    Wear PPE and light the smoker. The smoker should produce cool, steady smoke. Stand out of the
        main flight line.
  4.    Open gently. Apply only modest smoke as needed. Lift roof and crownboard carefully; avoid
        sudden bangs or crushing bees.
  5.    Make working space. Remove an outer frame when safe and put it in a clean frame holder or box.
        Keep comb covered where practical.

  6.    Work frame by frame over the open brood box. Hold frames upright and secure; examine both
        faces in good light. Look for stores, eggs, larvae, sealed brood, drone brood, queen cells and
        abnormal symptoms.
  7.    Check the brood nest especially carefully in swarm season. Queen cells can be hidden along side
        bars or lower edges. If you find charged cells, make a deliberate swarm-control decision, not a
        routine box-closing gesture.
  8.    Handle only what is necessary. Do not leave uncapped brood in cold wind or intense sun. Be
        particularly careful if the queen is on the frame.
  9.    Rebuild in the correct order. Maintain frame spacing, ensure no bees are trapped under box edges,
        replace any queen excluder correctly, close crownboard and roof, and secure the hive.
  10. Record immediately. Date, weather, queen evidence, brood health, stores, space, cells,
      temperament, mite check, actions and next date. If medicines were used, complete the separate
      statutory medicine record.

During peak swarm conditions, strong colonies often need inspections at about seven-day intervals,
sometimes sooner according to risk and method. That is an operational guide, not a reason to expose
brood in dangerous weather. Check the BBKA beekeeping-year guidance and your mentor’s local advice.

Holding a frame well

Hold frame lugs firmly but gently; rotate through the vertical plane rather than flipping a heavy comb
horizontal in bright sun, which risks breaking fresh wax. Keep the frame above the hive so a queen or
cluster that falls is more likely to return to the brood chamber. Learn to read a whole frame: central sealed
brood, open brood nearer the new laying edge, pollen rim and nectar stores. Use sunlight from behind
your shoulder to see eggs, not a torch pressed against the comb.

What to record and when to act

A useful record is brief and consistent: ‘Queen seen?’ is less important than ‘eggs and all brood stages?’.
Grade food as light/adequate/heavy, note actual frame count for stronger detail, record charged/swarm
cells explicitly and write one next action with a date. Avoid intervention based on one ambiguous
observation; where foulbrood is suspected, however, stop movement and notify without delay.
